euthyphro raises  a central question which a person could claim to "2. In my view, this question is so powerful that it can dismantle traditional popular understanding of conventional religion.": is a thing good because it is pious, or is it pious because it is fundamnetaly good

this question recurs throughout the philosophy of religion: is the concept of morality or of general 'goodness' (whether it be rational, aetshetic, moral etc) caused by the gods, or do they somehow trump the gods in their fundamentality? basically, the question can be reduced to whether goodness or godliness is more 'axiomatic' to the universe.

the answer to the question can lead to fundamental rifts in the worldview of religion:
- if the former is true, then gods are the ultimate authority -> piety should be an essential part of human (Kant)
- if the latter is true, then gods are not the ultimate authority: rather they are mere 'verifiers' of good quality- instead the inherent goodness of things (however one wishes to judge that) is the ultimate authority -> good judgement should be an essential part of human (what Kierkegaard and Sartre implicitly suggest in their existentialist ideals)

- processes make things good or bad, not their initial point - hence judgement is more important and things can become good whilst not bering initiallyu pious (pious is an initial judgement that persists, whereas goodness is a thing that can transform and become asnd iterate) (borrowed from the process theoidiucy for the problem of evil) ->>>> latter side

- actions aligning with the moral intuition are performed continually by people who would not believe gods word- hence moral intu
- what about necessary evil? the problem of evil forces one to admit that god created evil in order to service a greater good, whatever that might be. hence, as evil is created by gods and inevitable in his cosmic alugnment, it is pious to the degree it services gfods intentions (whatever that might be) -> hence, evil too is good?
